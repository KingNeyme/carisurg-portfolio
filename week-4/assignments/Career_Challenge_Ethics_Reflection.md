# Career Challenge: AI Ethics in Clinical Practice

## Article Summary

I reviewed the World Health Organization's 2021 guidance, *Ethics and Governance of Artificial Intelligence for Health*. The guidance argues that the promise of AI in diagnosis, treatment, research, and public health can only be realised when ethics and human rights are built into design, deployment, and use. It sets out six broad principles: protecting human autonomy; promoting well-being, safety, and the public interest; ensuring transparency and explainability; establishing responsibility and accountability; ensuring inclusiveness and equity; and promoting responsive, sustainable systems. My main takeaway is that safe AI is not only a modelling problem. It requires governance that makes developers, healthcare organisations, and public bodies answerable to the clinicians and communities affected by the technology.

## My Position

I support **AI augmentation for high-stakes clinical judgement** and carefully bounded automation for low-risk support tasks.

AI may automatically check whether vital signs are missing, stale, recorded in the wrong unit, or physiologically implausible. It may also identify duplicate records, prepare a queue for reassessment, or complete administrative quality checks, provided that failures are visible and staff can continue safely during downtime.

AI should not independently assign a final emergency triage category, decide that a patient can safely wait, or make admission and discharge decisions. These choices combine incomplete data, patient communication, examination, changing symptoms, local capacity, and professional accountability. The model may provide an urgency estimate and explain the factors that influenced it, but a qualified clinician must interpret that support and remain free to override it.

My uncertainty is about where the boundary may move. Strong prospective evidence could justify more automation for a narrow, stable task with clear inputs, reliable monitoring, and a safe fallback. I am not yet convinced that the same is true for broad triage decisions across varied Caribbean hospitals. The evidence, workflow, and accountability arrangements should determine the degree of automation, not the technical excitement around the model.

## Source

World Health Organization. *Ethics and governance of artificial intelligence for health*. Geneva: World Health Organization; 2021. ISBN: 9789240029200.
